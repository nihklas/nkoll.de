# Pre-flight preparations

The first thing I did was charge the batteries. I already ordered a new wallplug
for the WhoopStor charger as I didn't have a spare one lying around with enough
power. It needs at least 30 watts input power, so I took my beefy 96W MacBook
charger and use that one for the first few charging cycles. I have the `480 mAh`
Lava II batteries from BetaFPV, so to charge them in a way to not damage them
and keep them as long lived as possible I charge them with a current setting of
`0.4 A`. You could probably get away with a little more and therefore charge
them quicker but I wasn't in a hurry and I would rather play it safe until I
have more experience.

<Collapse>
    <Fragment slot="summary">
        Where does the `0.4 A` come from?
    </Fragment>

    Batteries have a few important measurements associated with them. They vary
    from type, capacity, use case, manufacturer and so on. The important one to
    charge is the capacity in milliamp-hours, in this case `480 mAh`.

    The other important measurement is the C-Rating. It describes the maximum
    discharge current the battery can safely provide. The Lava II's have a
    C-Rating of `95C`. This by itself is meaningless. To get the actual
    discharge current you have to multiply the capacity in amps by the C-Rating.
    To get from milliamp to amp you simply divide by `1000`.

    So in this case, the maximum current provided by the battery would be
    `(480 / 1000) * 95 = 45.6 A`. If you punch the throttle and make the drone's
    motors run at full power, the battery could provide at it's peak about `45 A`.

    This is for discharging. For charging a good rule of thumb is to charge at
    `1C`. That means, set the charging current to (in this case)
    `(480 / 1000) * 1 = 0.48 A`. The charger can set the charging current in
    `0.1 A` increments from anywhere between `0.3-1.5 A`. I rounded down and got
    to `0.4 A` for my charging current.

</Collapse>

While the batteries were charging, I connected the drone to my computer and used
`Betaflight Configurator` to configure a few things. You can give the aircraft a
name and my girlfriend and I named the little guy "Gary". The name is displayed
in the OSD (On-Screen-Display). I changed a few switches around so I can control
him like I want but most of the default settings are pretty good. It is a
Bind-N-Fly after all so the whole point is for the defaults to be good so you
can just _bind_ you controller and goggles and immediately start _flying_. I was
looking through the settings to see what else I can do until the batteries are
charged but there wasn't really something to change until I flew the drone
because most of these settings change the flight characteristics so until I
actually fly it, I can't really tell if I made it better or worse.

I bound my controller to the drone by pressing the "bind" button on the receiver
tab in Betaflight and then selecting "bind" in the ExpressLRS Lua Script on my
controller. For anyone that doesn't know what those mean, there are a lot of
easy to follow beginner tutorials for this setup but if you don't plan on flying
yourself these details don't matter. Just know that I bound the controller to
the drone. After that I also bound the goggles. The VTX (Video-Transmitter) of
the drone is analog, which means it just broadcasts a signal through the air on
a specific frequency and to "bind" the goggles you basically just have to set
the goggles to the same frequency and it should show you the video. Most goggles
have an auto-find feature that just scan all frequencies and choose the one with
the strongest signal. Power the drone right next to the goggles, choose the
auto-find and you should easily find the signal. That's it, now you can go and
fly FPV!

# at last, i fly

As soon as the batteries were fully charged, I plugged them into Gary and
started flying. I have the freestyle version of the BetaFPV Air75 II, which has
the least powerful motors. That doesn't mean it's weak though. It is incredibly
powerful and fast and you really have to be careful to not bump into everything.
Especially the throttle is very sensitive and flying indoors means you basically
keep the throttle between 10-30% or you rocket into the ceiling. After the first
few attempts I plugged Gary back into the computer and set a throttle limit to
80%, which means the full throttle command from the controller means 80%
throttle for the motors. The whole scale in between also has more resolution and
_should_ make flying indoors a bit easier. It still was incredibly difficult.

<Collapse>
    <Fragment slot="summary">
        What's Angle mode and Acro mode?
    </Fragment>

    FPV drones have multiple different flight modes. The most common are Angle
    mode and Acro mode.

    Angle mode is similar to what you know from DJI Camera drones. Moving the
    right stick forward means the drone moves forward. Moving it backwards cause
    the drone to fly backwards. Left and Right the same. Centering the stick
    makes the drone level itself out. The difference to camera drones is that
    you still have to manage the throttle yourself, so there is no "move up or
    down" or "hold altitude" and you constantly adjust throttle strength
    yourself. Also, leveling out means the drone is not banking in any
    direction, if you have momentum from flying forward or you are hovering in
    the wind you are still going to drift away. There is no "position hold" as
    you might expect from DJI drones.

    Acro mode is complete manual control. The left stick behaves the same,
    throttle and yaw control is unchanged. The right stick however now controls
    the rotation of the aircraft. Moving the stick forward rotates the drone
    forward with no limit on how far it goes. This is what enables those flashy
    freestyle tricks. Centering the stick also doesn't level the drone out. Once
    the drone is rotated and you leave the stick alone, it stays in the
    orientation you put it. This is very difficult to control and get into but
    gives you by far the most control. In my opinion, it's also way more fun to
    fly.

    There are other flight modes but they are typically not used.

</Collapse>

The first flying experiences were amazing. It was pretty difficult to get the
throttle control going indoors but once you get it, it is an incredible feeling
flying through your apartment and seeing everything either from very low or very
high. Seeing yourself sitting there basically not moving with the goggles on and
the controller in hand is weird but in a good way. You feel like you left your
body and are actually inside the drone. It feels both like a video game and an
out-of-body experience. Flying in front of a mirror and seeing the little drone
looking back at you is especially fun.

After a few days of flying angle mode I started my first real try at acro mode.
Immediately I felt like I never learned how to fly the drone and the simulator
lied to me. It was completely different. Also the default rates that BetaFPV set
for this quad were not suited for my liking. I quickly got back into angle mode
to finish this pack and then connected the drone back to my computer and changed
my rates. I looked up what other people fly their tinywhoops with for freestyle
and copied the rates from [this blog post](https://oscarliang.com/best-tinywhoop-settings/#My-Whoop-Rates-Acro-Mode).

<Collapse>
    <Fragment slot="summary">
        What are rates?
    </Fragment>

    You can tune how much stick input correlates to how much movement of the
    drone. Those functions are called rates.

    They consist of 3 main parts, center sensitivity, max rate and expo.

    Center sensitivity accounts for the responsiveness around the stick center.
    Lower value enables smoother, finer adjustments, larger value makes small
    movements more responsive and twitchy.

    Max rate is the amount of degrees per seconds the drone tries to spin at
    when at full stick deflection. The higher the number, the faster it spins.
    For example, 360 means 360 degrees per seconds or one full rotation per
    second. 720 therefore means 2 full rotations per second.

    Expo gives the rate a curve. A larger value gives the center sensitivity a
    larger window to operate but causes the change in speed around the edges to
    have big differences. This can cause unpredictable changes in
    responsiveness. A low expo makes the function more linear and the speed
    gradually increases from center to edge. That can lose you some finer
    control in the middle though.

    "Tuning the rates" refers to the process of trying out different setups to
    find the best rates for a specific pilot and quad. I haven't tuned them, I
    just copied them from the mentioned blog post and am very happy so far. I am
    not yet good enough to try my hands on actually tuning them, that'll be
    something to do in the future.

</Collapse>

That changed the way the drone handles is much more similar to what I got used
to in the sim. I tried carefully flying around the flat, avoiding the cat's
water bowls and most of the walls but I did bump into a lot of things and
crashed more times than I could count. But as the saying goes, mistakes are what
you learn from. Through sheer will power and a lot of practice in a short amount
of time I got used to flying the small drone in a small apartement in acro mode.
I also started doing full rolls and flips to start my freestyle journey.
Orbiting around my ceiling lamp is pretty easy and one of the first tricks I
naturally did. Doing rolls and flips as a lot harder than that. You have to time
the stick inputs very precise to not overshoot the roll and begin throttling up
just when you are up right again. It's difficult but fun. Rolls are easier than
flips because you can see the horizon at all times, with flips you have to go
purely from timing and muscle memory. Anyways, what I'm trying to say is, flying
freestyle is hard, like very hard, but even more fun than it is difficult. And
crashing is part of the process. I crashed so much, sometimes pretty bad, but
the worst that happened so far is one blade of one propeller is slightly bend,
which causes vibrations in the camera. I can bend it back to roughly where it
was and the vibrations are gone and the quad flies normal. That thing is rock
solid!

# Final thoughts of the first days

I love it! Flying FPV is one of the most fun I had over multiple days since a
long time. It is definitely a hobby I want to keep doing. There is also very
little holding me back with my current setup. Even though I thought that only 4
battery packs for the drone would be to little, considering you typically only
get 5-7 minutes of flight per pack, for flying at home or anywhere you can
immediately charge them back up, this is not a limiting factor. You will crash
and pick up the drone sometimes more than you fly it and having a break in
between sessions is also good since flying non stop is pretty exhausting. I will
get more batteries anyways because when you are outside at a spot you want to
fly at, you typically don't want to fly just 30 minutes and then wait for
another 20-40 minutes for your first pack to charged back up. Having at least 8
packs will probably give the first pack enough time to charge while flying the
others to charge in the mean time and for me to not get any significant downtime
waiting for batteries. Another thing I noticed after flying for a few days is
that the VR04 goggles are pretty front heavy and the strap is not that great. I
will probably get a good replacement strap for a better fit on the head. But
with the drone itself, the controller, even the goggles in general I have no
problems at all, quite the opposite. I am very happy with my setup and can only
recommend it to anyone who wants to get into FPV on a budget. I am still below
400 Euros and I am having the time of my life.

As soon as I get better I also want to try my hands at some basic video editing
to get my DVR footage nicely cut and put some music underneath so I can post my
progress on social media. I will still keep the blog posts going and will link
any significant footage I deem blog worthy.

Any questions about FPV flying, getting into the hobby etc, feel free to contact
me. I won't be able to answer everything, I'm still a beginner, but I'll try my
best to help and get as many people into the hobby as I can.
