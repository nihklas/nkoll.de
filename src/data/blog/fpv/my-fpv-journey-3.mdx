# Still waiting...

The most difficult part for any order you are excited about. I regularly updated
the tracking website to find out where my package is at the moment. I got
increasingly unpatient even though I knew it could take a while. BetaFPV's
shipping policy says 12-30 days for standard international shipping and they
almost got to the upper end of that promise. 27 Days after ordering it finally
arrived. In the meantime I swapped between normal day-to-day life, checking the
tracking site and flying in the simulator. I had to wait for like a week until I
even got the tracking link. The tracking sometimes didn't update so it looked
like the package got stuck at some station and a few days later it was already
in another country. For anyone as impatient as I am, ordering from China is a

# Pre-flight preparations

The first thing I did was charge the batteries. I already ordered a new wallplug
for the WhoopStor charger as I didn't have a spare one lying around with enough
power. It needs at least 30 watts input power, so I took my beefy 96W MacBook
charger and use that one for the first few charging cycles. I have the `480 mAh`
Lava II batteries from BetaFPV, so to charge them in a way to not damage them
and keep them as long lived as possible I charge them with a current setting of
`0.4 A`. You could probably get away with a little more and therefore charge
them quicker but I wasn't in a hurry and I would rather play it safe until I
have more experience.

    <Fragment slot="summary">
        Where does the `0.4 A` come from?
    </Fragment>

    Batteries have a few important measurements associated with them. They vary
    from type, capacity, use case, manufacturer and so on. The important one to
    charge is the capacity in milliamp-hours, in this case `480 mAh`.

    The other important measurement is the C-Rating. It describes the maximum
    discharge current the battery can safely provide. The Lava II's have a
    C-Rating of `95C`. This by itself is meaningless. To get the actual
    discharge current you have to multiply the capacity in amps by the C-Rating.
    To get from milliamp to amp you simply divide by `1000`.

    So in this case, the maximum current provided by the battery would be
    `(480 / 1000) * 95 = 45.6 A`. If you punch the throttle and make the drone's
    motors run at full power, the battery could provide at it's peak about `45 A`.

    This is for discharging. For charging a good rule of thumb is to charge at
    `1C`. That means, set the charging current to (in this case)
    `(480 / 1000) * 1 = 0.48 A`. The charger can set the charging current in
    `0.1 A` increments from anywhere between `0.3-1.5 A`. I rounded down and got
    to `0.4 A` for my charging current.

While the batteries were charging, I connected the drone to my computer and used
`Betaflight Configurator` to configure a few things. You can give the aircraft a
name and my girlfriend and I named the little guy "Gary". The name is displayed
in the OSD (On-Screen-Display). I changed a few switches around so I can control
him like I want but most of the default settings are pretty good. It is a
Bind-N-Fly after all so the whole point is for the defaults to be good so you
can just _bind_ you controller and goggles and immediately start _flying_. I was
looking through the settings to see what else I can do until the batteries are
charged but there wasn't really something to change until I flew the drone
because most of these settings change the flight characteristics so until I
actually fly it, I can't really tell if I made it better or worse.

I bound my controller to the drone by pressing the "bind" button on the receiver
tab in Betaflight and then selecting "bind" in the ExpressLRS Lua Script on my
controller. For anyone that doesn't know what those mean, there are a lot of
easy to follow beginner tutorials for this setup but if you don't plan on flying
yourself these details don't matter. Just know that I bound the controller to
the drone. After that I also bound the goggles. The VTX (Video-Transmitter) of
the drone is analog, which means it just broadcasts a signal through the air on
a specific frequency and to "bind" the goggles you basically just have to set
the goggles to the same frequency and it should show you the video. Most goggles
have an auto-find feature that just scan all frequencies and choose the one with
the strongest signal. Power the drone right next to the goggles, choose the
auto-find and you should easily find the signal. That's it, now you can go and
fly FPV!

# at last, i fly

As soon as the batteries were fully charged, I plugged them into Gary and
started flying. I have the freestyle version of the BetaFPV Air75 II, which has
the least powerful motors. That doesn't mean it's weak though. It is incredibly
powerful and fast and you really have to be careful to not bump into everything.
Especially the throttle is very sensitive and flying indoors means you basically
keep the throttle between 10-30% or you rocket into the ceiling. After the first
few attempts I plugged Gary back into the computer and set a throttle limit to
80%, which means the full throttle command from the controller means 80%
throttle for the motors. The whole scale in between also has more resolution and
_should_ make flying indoors a bit easier. It still was incredibly difficult.
