# I did not wait

[In my last post](/posts/fpv/my-fpv-journey-1) I said that I would wait 2 weeks
to see if the simulator keeps being fun before I buy the radio. Well, I bought
it a bit earlier. I bought it together with batteries for the radio and the
charger for the drone batteries as they were all from the same store. The
charger wasn't in stock at the time so the package got delayed anyways. Paired
with some holidays, this caused the package to take a few days longer than
usual. It arrived about 5 days after ordering. Immediately I started going back
into the simulator, but with my new Radiomaster Pocket Crush instead of my PS5
controller. Also, just a day after ordering the radio (so even before it arrived)
I also ordered the rest of my starting equipment. I ordered it directly from the
manufacturer in China instead of an international or european store because even
after tax, shipping, conversion etc, I still saved about 50-100 € on the whole
order. The drawback when ordering from the manufacturer is the shipping time. It
still hasn't arrived and I am waiting for almost two weeks. Just today I got the
shipping confirmation and tracking number so I am expecting to wait for at least
another week or two.

# Simulator Training

Since I got the radio, I immediately started going back into the simulator and
tried flying with it. Setting the radio itself up to work with it was
suprisingly easy. I looked up a little guide to set the controller up in
general, stuff like display brightness, battery warning thresholds and other
system settings. To set it up for working with a simulator, you basically create
a new profile (they are called "models" in the operating system of the radio)
where you disable all the transmitters, since they would be outputting signals
to nowhere and drain the batteries. Then it was as easy as connecting a USB-C
cable and selecting Joystick Mode on the prompt that appeared on the radio. It
immediately got recognized by Liftoff (the simulator in question) and I could
fly without any additional setup. Later on I also configured in Liftoffs
settings a switch on the controller for arming and disarming the drone so I
could also practice actually landing the aircraft (something way more difficult
than it should be).

At first it was very weird flying with a real controller made for flying real
drones. The sticks are way longer than on a typical gamepad and they move
further. Also, the throttle axis doesn't center on itself, meaning wherever you
put the throttle at, it will stay there and you don't have to constantly put
pressure on the stick which caused lots of imprecision and adjustments when I
still flew with the PS5 controller. The bigger sticks also meant that
fast/acrobatic flying now involves more pronounced movements of my hands. This
was the hardest thing to get used to, as I still needed to figure out how to
hold my hands and the sticks the best way so I could even reach the edges of the
gimbals comfortably. On the other hand, slow flying was on a different level, as
the bigger gimbals meant I now have the ability to do incredibly small and
precise movements compared to the gamepad. As of writing this, I have a total of
42 hours of playtime in Liftoff, about half of which were with the PS5
controller and the other half with the Radiomaster Pocket. I feel pretty
comfortable in flying in a controlled and pretty fast manner, at least in the
simulator.

I am currently grinding all the races on the different maps to the point where I
want to finish each race at least once in first place. Everytime I finish a
Race, I also save the recording in game so I can go back and hopefully see a
progression when I come back at a later time and try to improve my lap times. I
also try a little bit of freelight now and then to practice some freestyle
tricks because that is where I want to go with my flying. I am mostly interested
in that, since that is what actually got me interested in the hobby. Doing cool
and flashy lines of freestyle tricks is harder than you think, even when you
already know that is harder than it looks. Those pilots you see on social media
make it look so easy and fun, that's where I want to get to. When the real drone
arrives I also plan on recording the DVR (which is basically a recording inside
the goggles of the image I am seeing) and posting it on Instagram and here on
future blog posts. For now, the simulator recordings are good enough for me.

**Some notes:**

- this does not include shipping fees
- Prices are as of date of ordering, so this is exactly what I paid but the
  prices may vary depending on when you look. Also, the prices from BetaFPVs own
  store don't directly use Euro. They are given as USD and converted by
  (presumably) the payment provider, in my case my bank. That means that you may
  pay more depending on the exchange rates and conversion fees your payment
  provider takes.
- The Lava batteries come in a pack of 4 (or 5 if you take the smaller capacity
  ones). In hindsight, that could be a little few and I will probably buy more
  batteries pretty soon. 4 batteries of this size would give me a total flight
  time of about 20-26 minutes. It's fine when I'm flying indoors as I can just
  charge them immediatley after, but when I am going outside, less than 30
  minutes of flying does seem a bit to short.

In my opinion, about 350 € is a good price for starting into the hobby. It will
get a lot more expensive with time and asking/googling around I feared of an
even higher barrier to entry. This is not the absolute cheapest way you can go,
but from what my research concluded, this is the cheapest but most
usable/best-value-for-money entry into the hobby. There are for example cheaper
radios, simulators, goggles, etc., you could build the drone yourself, you don't
really need the neck strap... The absolute bare minimum would be the sim and a
radio. Flying with a gamepad in the sim is possible but it feels a lot
different, more game-y and less simulation-y. Also, there are so called ready to
fly kits that package goggles, radio, drone and batteries into a complete
package that you unpack and immediately be able to fly. They _can_ be cheaper,
but the internet is pretty sure that if you aren't willing to pay at least what
I listed here, you will get basically garbage and don't experience the hobby in
a way that will keep you at it.

# What's next?

Obviously I will keep flying in the sim. As soon as the drone and other stuff
arrives, I will fly it in my apartement. I will also try to get my first DVR
recorded and posted in my next blog post. I am consuming so much content about
drones lately, mostly on YouTube or through blog posts. I am learning so much
and will keep on learning until I know everything, which we all now is
impossible. This is a very multifaceted hobby, there are so many different
things to focus on. Flying Style (Freestyle and it's many different sub-styles,
racing, longrange, cinematic, etc.), battery care and maintenance, soldering and
building a custom drone, different video systems (analog vs digital), different
drone classes (tinywhoop, cinewhoop, toothpick, x-frame, deadcat, 2", 3", 5" and
so on). You go as deep into the rabbit hole as you want. Depending on your goal,
there is also a bit or regulatory stuff you need to know about so you don't get
in trouble with the law.

When I am writing Part 3, I will hopefully already have quite a bit of flight
time with the real drone and can document my experience on the real deal. Until
then, I will keep on practicing and learning what there is to learn.
